Every recipe is a fact you can check

Loam is a crafting game. You drag two things together and get a third, and the third one is true — it carries the mechanism that makes it work and a source you can open. This is the guide to how that is done and how it is kept honest.

The claim

Checkable, not infallible

The genre this game belongs to is fun and invented. Little Alchemy and Doodle God make their chemistry up; Infinite Craft generates endless plausible nonsense with a language model. Loam takes the opposite bet: every step is a real physical or historical fact, and every fact carries a source you can open from inside the game.

The claim is checkability, not infallibility, and the difference is the whole point. Auditing our own confident, fluent, well-written prose against primary reporting found close to half of it wrong on first pass — a greenhouse described as trapping infrared when a real one works by blocking convection, flint credited with obsidian's edge, a penicillin death-toll figure that traces back to a magazine ranking. Every one of those sentences read beautifully.

That is the argument for this project, and it is an argument against trusting anyone's prose, including ours. So the sources are machine-verified against live articles, the numbers in the sentences are checked against the articles they cite, and the parts that have been independently audited say so.

Playing

Two gestures, and only two

Everything in the game is one of two moves. Drag a card onto another card to merge them; drag a card onto a verb tool to process it. Merges are commutative — order never matters. Processes are unary, one thing in, one thing out.

Verbs unlock through play, which recapitulates tech history in miniature. Time, force and cold need no technology, so wait, crush and chill are there from the first move. Heat arrives with fire, cut with flint, ferment with yeast.

The bench

Cards you are working with sit on the bench. It holds far more than fits on screen: you can fold cards away without deleting them, and the visible limit and the total limit are separate numbers, so tidying never costs you your work. Double-tap clears a card. Search finds anything by name, by any of its everyday names, or by its id.

Playing

The specimen card

Nothing in this game is an emoji and nothing is a rounded pill, which are the two signatures of the genre. Emoji is somebody else's art, it renders differently on every platform, and it cannot draw a peptide bond. Every item is a specimen card instead: a fixed portrait frame, so a rock and a cake occupy the same footprint and are told apart by what is in it.

The molecular tier gets its own drawing language — IUPAC skeletal convention and Jmol/RasMol atom colours — because that is what a chemist expects to see, and the switch marks the point where you leave the world of things.

Playing

Two shelves, and one of them is marked by an absence

Workshop recipes are real. They carry a this is how it works card at the moment of discovery and a source you can open. Folklore recipes are myths — Stone Soup, the Philosopher's Stone, the Golden Egg — and they are marked not by a colour or a label but by what is missing: no accession number, no source, a dashed edge.

Marking the difference by absence rather than by a badge is both the better media literacy lesson and the one that survives colour blindness. You keep the whimsy the genre is loved for, quarantined so it never contaminates the teaching.

356 of 7,844 elements sit on the folklore shelf.

Building

How a thing gets its category

An element is filed four independent ways, and they answer four different questions. Keeping them separate is deliberate: each one is checkable on its own, and a disagreement between two of them is a signal rather than a nuisance.

  • Shelf — is this real or is it a story? Two values, workshop and folklore, and nothing else. It decides whether a source is required.
  • Tags — what kind of thing is it? A small closed vocabulary. Tags are what the shelf families are built from, and they are the layer a person authors.
  • Taxon — for a living thing, what is it? A pointer into taxonomy.json, which is a strict hierarchy with one parent per group, because real rank is. Composition — what a thing is made of — is a separate tree in bedrock.json, and it is a graph, not a hierarchy.
  • Scale — how big is it? An order of magnitude in metres, which also sorts the corpus into tiers: past 10⁻⁸ m you have left the world of things and the drawing language changes with you.

The shelf families are the drawers you actually browse in the game, and they come from data/families.json — one file read by the game and by the checker, so the two cannot drift apart. That file also holds the vocabulary: any tag not in it is an error, not a new category.

Checking

The mechanisms

A mechanism is a question asked of the whole corpus at once, in a form a machine can repeat. They are the reusable part of this project — more reusable than the content — because each one encodes a way a body of facts can be wrong that a person reading it one item at a time would never see.

They fall into four kinds. Some are gates: the build does not pass without them. Some measure completeness, which is the only way to find a hole where a whole subject should be. Some check structure rather than wording. And some are about craft — whether you can see a thing, tell it from its neighbour, and find it when you look.

The gate

Nothing ships past these. They fail the build.

validate

node tools/validate.mjs

Is the graph intact, and can you actually reach everything from four rocks?

Walks the corpus for schema faults — a recipe with two inputs and a verb, an input that names nothing, two different gestures producing the same thing — and then plays the game: it starts with the four starters and expands, respecting the order the verbs unlock in, until nothing new appears. Anything left unreached is unreachable in play, not merely awkward.

What it caught. Reachability is the property that is easy to break and impossible to see by eye. A duplicate-name merge once dropped 88 recipes and stranded 6,037 elements; validate named it in one run, and the change was reverted rather than shipped.

7,844 of 7,844 reachable. 0 elements are made by nothing (starters aside).

sources

node tools/sources.mjs

Does the article we cite exist, and is it the one we think it is?

No URL in this repository was typed by a person. data/sources.json holds article titles; the tool resolves each against the live Wikipedia API, follows redirects, and refuses to write anything it could not fetch. Only then does apply put URLs into recipes.json.

What it caught. On its first run it caught one title that did not exist and four that silently redirected somewhere else. It still reports redirects on every run — Percolozoa now answers to Heterolobosea, Aquifoliaceae to Holly — which is how a citation quietly stops pointing at what it claimed to. Resolutions are cached for thirty days: re-asking about every title on every run had grown to twenty minutes of backoff against rate limits, and a check that expensive is a check a content batch quietly skips.

9,764 of 9,764 recipes carry a resolved source (100%).

audit

node tools/audit.mjs

Does the number in our sentence appear in the article we cited for it?

Pulls every figure, date and named person out of the teaching text — and out of a recipe's temperature band, which is a numeric claim like any other — fetches the cited article, and looks for them. A claim that cannot be found in its own source is not automatically wrong, but it is unsupported, and it is listed. Where the prose does not carry a fact the article's infobox is pulled as a second chance, because melting points and dimensions and dates live there rather than in the text.

What it caught. This is the mechanism the whole project rests on. Auditing our own confident, fluent, well-written prose against primary reporting found a first-pass error rate near half. A greenhouse does not work by trapping infrared; flint was given obsidian's record; film does not work by persistence of vision; Darwin coined "living fossil" about the platypus, not the coelacanth. All fluent. All wrong. And the check itself was wrong six ways — a comma after a year, a diacritic, a possessive, a chemical formula, an en dash, a stray quote mark — each of which made it report correct content as unsupported. Clearing those took the attribution count from 50 to 0 and made the remaining backlog worth reading. A check that is more than half noise is a check nobody finishes.

7,683 recipes independently verified, 1,685 article-supported.

Completeness

What is owed that has not been written yet.

needs

node tools/needs.mjs

If a thing has a real parts list, do we have all the parts?

A battery needs an anode, a cathode, an electrolyte and a separator. A house needs a foundation before it needs a finish. Each list is authored from a real source and the tool checks the corpus against it, allowing a|b where either part genuinely satisfies the need.

What it caught. Closing these lists is what turned scattered plausible items into things that hold together: a loom with no heddle is a word, not a loom.

1,002 of 1,002 components present across 76 lists (100%).

universe

node tools/universe.mjs

Is there a hole where a whole subject should be?

A checklist of the concepts an educated person would expect a world to contain, grouped into domains — the tree of life, the human body, materials and making, mathematics and logic, society and economy. It is a coverage map, not a quality measure: it says a subject is represented, never that it is represented well.

What it caught. Whole domains were missing before this existed. It is the mechanism that turns "we have a lot of elements" into "we have no gap where mathematics should be".

589 of 589 concepts present, across 23 domains.

verbs

node tools/verbs.mjs

For every element, do we know what each verb does to it — and at what temperature?

The other checks look at what exists. This one looks at what is owed. An expectation table encodes physical rules — everything organic ferments or rots, everything solid can be crushed, anything with water in it freezes — and the tool multiplies those rules across the corpus to produce a bill. --bands then asks a harder question of heat alone: a temperature band is what lets one element heat to several different products, and a heat recipe without one is claiming there is only one answer.

What it caught. It immediately exposed a fault class nothing else could see: recipes where the verb is real and the output is real but the arrow between them is association, not derivation. Heating gold does not make a crown; it makes molten gold, and a crown is what a smith does next. A bell is cast from bronze, not heated into being.

979 verb outcomes written over 7,844 elements. 71 heat recipes state a temperature. 24 gestures are established as doing nothing, with the reason recorded.

places

node tools/places.mjs

Is this a place, or a category with a proper noun stuck on it — and is the corpus of places actually global?

A landmark is the one kind of element where being generically true is a failure, and it passes every other check here: the sentence is true, the source resolves, the drawing is distinct, the scale is right. So this asks two things nothing else can. Deep — does each place carry the four facts that make it that place: when, who, what of, and why there? Global — measured against a world checklist of 334 sites across the 22 UN M49 subregions, because coverage measured against what you happen to have is not coverage, it is a total, and it will report a corpus of European castles as complete.

What it caught. Landmarks fail the same way heat recipes did. The Colosseum came from stone + lion — a lion is a thing that was in it, not a thing it is made of; it is faced in travertine carted twenty miles from Tibur and pinned with iron cramps, and one side is missing because the cramps were robbed out for their metal. The Great Wall came from china + mongolia. The Ishtar Gate came from clay and glass with nobody to build it. Five association faults in twenty places, in a set nothing else had ever flagged.

288 of 334 checklist places exist (86%), across 22 regions. 333 of 333 carry all four facts.

Structure

Whether the shape of a claim is right, not just its wording.

derivation

node tools/derivation.mjs

Does this organism actually come from the thing we say it comes from?

Reads the taxonomy and checks that a living thing's recipe is a real ancestry or a real ecology, not a rhyme. It is honest about its own reach: everything without a taxon, and every non-organism, is untested.

What it caught. A clean run here does not mean the corpus derives. It means the part of it that can be checked mechanically does — which is a smaller and more useful claim.

1,151 elements carry a taxon, across 617 groups.

backbone

node tools/backbone.mjs

Is the family tree a tree?

Every taxonomic group has exactly one parent — real rank is a strict hierarchy, unlike the composition tree, which is a graph. The tool looks for rank inversion (a child ranked at or above its parent), dead groups with no children and nothing tagged to them, and thin branches where a whole rank has one child.

What it caught. Rank inversions and dead groups both sit at zero now. What it still reports are thin branches, which are not bugs — they are the next place to look.

617 groups, used by 1,151 elements.

reactions

node tools/reactions.mjs

What kind of physical process is this, really?

Reads the mechanism out of each recipe's own teaching text and classifies it — dissolution, phase change, combustion, enzymatic action — so the game can show the category on the payoff ring rather than treating every merge as the same event.

What it caught. Naming the process is what stops a merge being magic. It also gives the art and the interface something true to colour by.

792 gestures classified.

safety

node tools/safety.mjs

Have we accidentally written a method?

Hazard text is the one place where being helpful is dangerous. The tool checks for instructional voice, for hazard chains that read as steps, and for guarantor language — "safe", "harmless", "you can" — that promises something no data file is entitled to promise.

What it caught. The policy it enforces: never phrase a hazard as a method, and never let a badge outrun the evidence. Only IARC Group 1 gets the carcinogen badge; 2A, 2B and 3 get words and no badge, because a badge is a claim and those groups do not support it.

343 hazards covering 621 elements.

homonyms

node tools/homonyms.mjs

Does this word mean here what the sentence beside it thinks it means?

An element id carries no sense. So data/homonyms.json gives each ambiguous word the meaning this corpus uses for it, and the words that would betray a different one; a recipe using that element whose why contains one of them is failing, because the prose and the gesture are then talking about different things. A heuristic was tried first — flag inputs whose outputs scatter across families — and it returned 684 candidates of which about two were real, so the file is curated rather than inferred, and the coverage number counts what a person has actually looked at.

What it caught. scale in this corpus is the weighing balance: pivoted beam, a pan at each end, Egyptian weighing stones from about 2600 BCE. Four recipes used it anyway — to make a moth's wing, a reptile's back, a shark's hide and the graduations on a ruler. bulb is the plant's, and three recipes lit a street with it. wave is the water one, and light was being made from a photon and a ripple. set is the Egyptian god of chaos, on the folklore shelf, and a mathematical function was being derived from him. Every one of those why texts was about the right thing. Only the gesture underneath was about something else — which is this project's own fault class, sitting in the middle of the corpus behind prose good enough to hide it.

77 words watched, 23 of which were actually wrong when the list was written. 327 of 1,637 single-word names reviewed (20%).

roles

node tools/roles.mjs

What is this noun to the thing being made — the stuff, the method, or the reason?

An element's tags say what it belongs to. They never say what part it plays when something is made from it. So data/roles.json gives each element one curated role: five that contribute to a making — material, technique, process, tool, maker — and five that do not — site, form, occupant, subject, evidence. With that field written down, "does this recipe say how the thing was made?" stops being a guess about tag vocabulary and becomes a lookup. An input with no role recorded is reported as unjudgeable rather than as a fault, because a partial file is only honest if the tools reading it say where it runs out.

What it caught. Marble and Mausolus are both inputs to the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus. One is the stone it is built of; the other is the man it was built for, and Artemisia II is the one who built it. No tag in this corpus tells those three apart, which is why the tag-based version of this check flagged 65 of 334 places and, read through by hand, found nothing wrong with any of them. The role field found 62 — all of them real. Mount Fuji was made from stratovolcano + japan while its own prose said it "builds itself out of its own ash and lava"; Hōryū-ji came from pagoda + dendrochronology, which dated the temple rather than building it; Chartres from cathedral + fire; Nazca from geoglyph + peru. In almost every case the sentence was already right and the gesture underneath it named something else — the same fault class as the homonym list, one level up.

369 elements carry a role, covering every input to a place recipe.

Craft

Whether you can see it, tell it apart, and find it.

categories

node tools/categories.mjs

Is every element in a drawer somebody would actually open?

Reads data/families.json — the same file the game reads — and files every element the way the game does. Then it checks the vocabulary is closed, counts what reaches the catch-all, lists elements that match two drawers, and cross-examines each tag against the element's own independently authored scale and taxon. That last check is the one that finds mistakes rather than untidiness: scale and taxonomy are written by different passes, so when they disagree with a tag, one of the three is wrong.

What it caught. The family table used to live only inside the renderer, where no tool could reach it. One element in seven — 1,075 of them — fell through it into a drawer called Other, and nothing counted them, because the renderer is the one part of the project that never reports what it could not file. The evidence check then found 287 tags its own data contradicted: keratin, dopamine and collagen filed as animals; DNA, ribosomes and the twenty amino acids filed as microbes; 244 evolutionary and epidemiological concepts filed as microbes because that tag had quietly become the drawer for all of biology. It also caught a bug in itself — a lookup that read an array as a map, so the taxonomy rules silently passed on every element for a whole run.

7,844 elements filed across 18 drawers, 0 in the catch-all.

art

node tools/art.mjs check

Can you tell these two drawings apart at shelf size — and can you see them at all?

Fingerprints every drawing and fails on any pair inside a category too alike to distinguish. A second check catches shapes painted in the card's own colour, or buried inside another shape of the same colour, which is invisibility rather than similarity.

What it caught. Nineteen real collisions on the first run, including five identical drink glasses and a hexagonal sulfur that should have been an S8 crown. The colour check later caught 160 mineral drawings silently blanked by a palette fix that moved a token onto the hairline colour — a fault introduced by a fix, which is exactly the kind a person never finds.

8,078 drawings, all present.

palette

node tools/palette.mjs

Where did this colour come from?

Reads the Munsell renotation data — the dataset behind the soil colour book soil scientists carry into the field — and converts each notation to sRGB through a documented pipeline: xyY under Illuminant C, to XYZ, Bradford-adapted to D65, to sRGB. Lighter and darker tints come from the same hue page, never from lightening a hex.

What it caught. It also refuses collisions: a tint may not land on the card colour, the hairline, the ink or the ground. When the only remaining step on a hue page is unusable it will step chroma up rather than quietly return a colour that disappears.

10 category colours and 14 surfaces, every one a measured chip.

scale

node tools/scale.mjs

How big is it?

Assigns every item an order of magnitude in metres, which the card prints under the drawing. It is the line that makes a corpus containing both a carbon atom and the sun legible as one collection.

What it caught. The buckets are not a continuous range — there is no 10⁻¹⁴ — and three content batches were silently dropped before the build named the missing bucket rather than skipping it.

7,844 items sized, spanning 10⁻¹⁰ m to 10⁹ m — 20 orders of magnitude.

redundancy

node tools/redundancy.mjs

Is there more than one way to make this?

Counts routes per element and finds the single-route chokepoints that gate the most of the graph. A game where everything has exactly one recipe is a puzzle with one solution and no room to be stuck in.

What it caught. The ratio is the honest number here and it is not good yet: a large majority of made elements have exactly one route.

1.24 recipes per made element. 6,308 of 7,843 are single-route.

discover

node tools/discover.mjs

How many blind guesses does a discovery actually cost?

Simulates a player against the real graph under different assist policies. Discoverability is the design risk that gets worse as the corpus grows, because every element added makes the haystack bigger faster than it adds needles.

What it caught. Tag-affinity nudging — the intuitive answer — was simulated and rejected: it stalls early, because the best recipes cross categories on purpose. The assist that survived is a secrets-remaining count, which narrows the field without ever naming a partner.

8,785 merges among 30,760,246 possible pairs.

Recording that nothing happens. A broad expectation rule bills for cutting hay, and cutting hay gives you shorter hay. Until that was written down it was indistinguishable from unwritten work, which made the backlog dishonest in the one direction that matters — it looked like there was more to do than there is. data/inert.json records where a verb genuinely does nothing and why, and the checker fails on any gesture listed there that also has a recipe. On its first run it caught five, and every one of the five recipes was right: churning is exactly crushing milk, and rain really does hydrolyse the feldspar out of granite.

The honest reading. A mechanism that reports 100% is reporting on its own checklist, not on the world. universe says a subject is represented, never that it is represented well; derivation says the checkable part checks out. Each tool states its own blind spot in its output, because a coverage number without a denominator you can inspect is the same fluent confidence this project exists to distrust.

Checking

Where the facts come from

No URL in this repository was written by hand. That is the one discipline that matters here, because a confident, plausible, non-existent citation is worse than no citation at all.

  1. A title, not a link. data/sources.json holds article titles. Authoring a claim means naming the article, never pasting a URL.
  2. Resolution. tools/sources.mjs check asks the live Wikipedia API for each title, follows redirects, and fails on anything that does not exist. It reports every redirect it followed, which is how a citation quietly stops pointing at what it claimed to.
  3. Application. Only after a clean resolve does apply write URLs into recipes.json. Where an encyclopedia article does not directly support a claim, the file takes a literal primary-source URL instead, checked by HTTP request.
  4. Audit. tools/audit.mjs pulls every number, date and named person out of the teaching text and looks for it in the article actually cited. Being in a source is a different claim from being cited by one.

Because of that last step, a claim carries a second flag beyond its source:

  • verified — independently checked against primary reporting. Shows a tick in the game. 7,683 recipes.
  • supported — a standard mechanism the cited article states directly: photosynthesis, osmosis, gluten. No independent search, and no tick. 1,685 recipes.

A correction to a recipe's mechanism text must also be applied to the paired codex fact on the element. Six codex entries were once left repeating errors already fixed in their recipes, which is an easy and completely invisible way to reintroduce a corrected claim.

Layers

Reading a real food label

You can point the game at something in your kitchen. It reads the ingredients off a real product and turns every one it recognises into a card, which is the moment the corpus stops being a game about the world and starts being about the shelf in front of you.

Safari has never implemented the browser's own barcode detector, and Apple requires every browser on iOS to use its engine, so on those devices camera scanning would simply not exist. It is powered by a vendored WebAssembly reader instead, which is why the feature works everywhere rather than only where the platform felt like supporting it.

What comes back is a real product record: name, brand, quantity, the ingredients text, the regulated allergens and traces the manufacturer declared, a nutrition panel per 100 g, and the three front-of-pack scores — Nutri-Score, NOVA and Eco-Score — each with its own explainer, because a letter grade with no stated formula is exactly the kind of confident claim this project is built to distrust.

Layers

Proteins, three ways

Protein shows up in three different places in this game, and they are genuinely different things that share a word.

  • The twenty amino acids, in bedrock.json, each with its three-letter and one-letter code, its residue and full formula, the atoms it is made of, and a sourced fact. The game groups them by side chain — nonpolar, polar, acidic, basic — because which group a residue belongs to is most of what it does: a protein folds the way it does because the nonpolar ones end up buried in the core. From there the corpus builds upward through dipeptide, polypeptide, alpha helix and beta sheet to a folded protein, and none of those steps is a metaphor.
  • The regulated allergens, in allergens.json, 14 of them. Each entry names the proteins, not the food — casein and beta-lactoglobulin rather than “milk” — because that is the fact the file exists to carry, and it is why an allergen survives being cooked, ground and blended into something unrecognisable. Each is recorded against the statute that requires declaring it: the US federal act as amended, and EU Regulation 1169/2011 Annex II.
  • Grams per hundred grams, from the scanned product's nutrition panel. That is a manufacturer's declared figure, shown as one row among the others, and it is the only one of the three that is a measurement rather than a molecule.

Nothing in the allergen layer says what anyone should eat or avoid. Every entry is a statement about a molecule or about a law, and the jurisdiction fields record what a statute requires — never what a body does.

Layers

Every colour is a measured chip

The palette is derived, not chosen. tools/palette.mjs reads the Munsell renotation data — the dataset behind the soil colour book that soil scientists carry into the field — and converts each notation to sRGB through a pipeline it records in the output file: xyY under Illuminant C, to XYZ, Bradford-adapted to D65, to sRGB.

Lighter and darker tints come from the same hue page rather than from lightening a hex, which is why they stay the same colour rather than becoming a paler version of a different one. Chroma is deliberately low, because real soil is low-chroma — the high-chroma warm brown that “earthy palette” has come to mean is not what earth looks like.

Two warm colours exist and the difference between them carries meaning. Accent, 5YR 6/6, is the interface: hover, focus, the current thing. Discovery, 5YR 6/12, is the only high-chroma colour in the game and it appears at exactly one moment — when something new exists. Used on a button, it would stop meaning anything.

Geometry never names a colour. Each shape carries a role and the theme decides what a role looks like, which is what makes the second theme — the same drawings unfilled, ink on paper — a stylesheet rather than a second set of 8,078 drawings.

A game whose claim is that everything in it is sourced, whose colours are also sourced, is a coherence a competitor cannot copy without copying the idea.

Building

How it is built and shipped

  1. The template is the source. prototype/template.html is edited; prototype/index.html is generated from it by tools/build-prototype.mjs, which bakes the graph in. Editing the built file is a change that disappears at the next build.
  2. The payload is split. The graph is inlined, because the file has to work from disk with no server and file:// blocks fetching. The two heaviest layers — the teaching prose and the drawings — are fetched separately afterwards and merged into the same objects, so the first paint does not wait for them.
  3. Validation gates the commit. tools/validate.mjs exits non-zero on any error and proves every element is still reachable from the four starters given the verb unlock order, which is the property that is easy to break and impossible to spot by eye.
  4. The repository is split in two. A public repository holds the game, the data and the tools; a private one holds strategy, research and the queue. The sync between them is an allow-list, never a deny-list — a deny-list fails silently the first time a new private file appears, and silence is not an acceptable failure mode when the consequence is publishing something meant to stay private.
